The A/B Test That "Failed" — and Why It Was My Favorite Result

Nobody builds a redesign hoping to hear "don't ship it." But sometimes that's exactly what the data says — and saying it clearly is the job.

The setup was simple. A software company had rebuilt its help website mobile-first, and the team was proud of it. Visitors on mobile devices were randomly split: half saw the original site, half saw the redesign. After browsing, each rated their satisfaction from 1 to 5. Over three weeks in July, 3,365 responses came in — 1,666 for the original, 1,699 for the new site. Clean groups, nearly identical sizes. A fair fight.

At first glance, the redesign was winning. The new site averaged 3.34 against the original's 3.28. If I had stopped there, the story writes itself: new site wins, ship it. A lot of dashboards stop there.

But a 0.06-point gap on a 5-point scale is exactly the kind of number that demands suspicion. I ran a two-sample Welch t-test — the variances differed slightly between groups, so the Welch adjustment was the honest choice. The p-value came back at 0.149. For the difference to be believable, I needed it under 0.05. Put plainly: if the two sites were truly identical, a gap this size would show up in roughly 15 out of 100 experiments purely by chance. Cohen's d landed at 0.05 — a negligible effect size. The redesign hadn't moved the experience.

p = 0.149 A "fail to reject" is not a failure. It's an answer.

The distribution told the more interesting story. The new site earned more 5-star ratings (19.1% vs 14.7%) — but also more 2-star ratings, and noticeably fewer 4-stars. Some users loved the redesign. Others were quietly put off by it. The averages canceled out into statistical silence.

My recommendation: don't deploy. Users already rated the existing site 3.3 out of 5, and replacing it with something unproven risks disrupting an experience that works — without evidence of a payoff. But I didn't leave the team empty-handed. That cluster of new 5-star ratings was a signal: something in the redesign genuinely delighted a segment of users. Find what that was, build the next iteration around it, and test again.

This is why the "failed" test is my favorite in my portfolio. Anyone can present a win. The real trust is earned when the data says not yet and you say it out loud — with a path forward attached.

2.1 Percentage Points: How Small Numbers Tell Big Stories

If I told you a new algorithm moved a metric from 14.29% to 16.35%, would you call it a breakthrough? Most rooms wouldn't. Most rooms would be wrong.

The question came from a video-feed experiment: could a new serving algorithm (Algorithm B) grow the share of "Engaged Watchers" — users who watch a meaningful amount of video — from a historical baseline of 14% toward a target of 17%? More engaged watchers means more video inventory, which means more ad revenue. The business case was directional and specific, so the test was too: a one-tailed hypothesis, declared before I looked at a single row of data. Lock in the decision rule first, or the data will happily tell you whatever you want to hear.

Before touching results, I ran a power analysis. The targeted improvement worked out to a minimum detectable effect of 21.4%, which at 5% significance and 80% power demands roughly 1,800 users per group. The experiment delivered: 1,819 in control, 1,792 in test. This step is unglamorous and routinely skipped — and skipping it is how teams run underpowered tests, see nothing, and wrongly conclude their idea didn't work.

The pivot table told the headline: Algorithm A converted 14.29% of its users into Engaged Watchers; Algorithm B converted 16.35%. A two-proportion z-test put the z-statistic at 1.716 and the one-tailed p-value at 0.043 — under the 0.05 bar. The lift was real.

+2.1pp = +14.4% Absolute lift looks small. Relative lift tells the story.

Here's where storytelling earns its keep. "Two point one percentage points" sounds like rounding error. But framed honestly: that's a 14.4% relative increase in the user behavior that drives video ad inventory — at the scale of a platform with billions of users. Small percentages on enormous denominators are how nine-figure revenue lines move. The same number, told two ways, produces two completely different decisions in the room.

My recommendation was to roll out Algorithm B fully, with post-launch monitoring to confirm the lift holds at scale and to watch for side effects on content diversity and session quality. A significant test result is a green light, not a blindfold.

The lesson I carry from this one: never let a number speak for itself, because it can't. 2.1 points is a shrug or a headline depending entirely on whether the analyst does the work of translation. That translation — from statistic to stakes — is the actual deliverable.

The €15,101 Margin: Telling the Truth About a Thin ROI

The flattering version of this analysis was sitting right there: referred users generated €762,072 — twice what non-referred users brought in. The honest version took four more steps.

An e-commerce referral program had paid €10 for every user who signed up with a referral code, across Germany, Spain, France, and Italy. Over a 28-day window: 14,329 users, 24,981 orders, €1.13M in revenue. Referred users were 63.8% of the user base and produced 67.3% of the revenue. If you wanted a victory-lap slide, you could stop right there.

But "referred users spent a lot" is not the question. The question is: what did the €91,400 in referral payouts buy that we wouldn't have gotten anyway? Those 9,140 people might have shopped regardless. To isolate the campaign's true effect, I built a counterfactual: what would referral users have generated if they behaved exactly like non-referral users?

The behavioral comparison surfaced the mechanism first. Referred users placed 16.1% more orders per person (1.836 vs 1.581) — a difference a two-sample t-test confirmed as decisively significant (t = 16.0, p < 0.0001). And crucially, their average order value was virtually identical: €45.42 vs €45.36. The campaign wasn't producing bigger baskets. It was producing repeat customers — the slower, compounding kind of loyalty.

The counterfactual math: had those 9,140 users purchased at the non-referral rate, they'd have generated €655,571. They actually generated €762,072. The campaign's incremental revenue — the part that exists only because of the behavior change — was €106,501. Subtract the €91,400 cost, and the true net return is €15,101.

16.52% ROI Real, positive — and only €1.65 per user away from zero.

A 16.52% return in 28 days is a win, and I reported it as one. But the same model that proved the win also exposed its fragility: the breakeven acquisition cost is about €11.65 per user. If payouts crept from €10 to €12, this campaign flips to a money-loser — and without the counterfactual, nobody would notice, because top-line referral revenue would still look spectacular.

So the story I delivered had three parts: the campaign works, the margin is thin, and the upside is bigger than the window — because a 16.1% purchase-frequency advantage, if it persists, compounds into lifetime value far beyond 28 days. Keep the program, hold the line on cost, and measure the cohort again at 90 days.

It would have been easy to lead with the doubled revenue and collect the applause. But an analysis that only finds good news isn't analysis — it's marketing wearing a lab coat. The thin margin was the insight.
